The Value of Happiness

This map shows the value of happiness of every nation.

It is the first where the suicide rate is counted into wellbeing, instead of being left out of it.

The figures on this map are real deaths. If you are going through a hard time, help is available. In the UK: Samaritans 116 123. In Italy: Telefono Amico 02 2327 2327. Elsewhere: findahelpline.com.

What this map is about

The World Happiness Report ranks countries on a single question, put to a sample of their residents: “on a scale from 0 to 10, where does your life stand today?”. The report calls the answer a life evaluation, and averages it over three years. It is a useful measure, but it cannot account for the people who chose to end that life.

Happiness value is not a measure anyone publishes. It is the composite built here, on this page, out of those two figures. The name is ours and so is the arithmetic; both are set out below.

The result is a tension known as the happiness–suicide paradox: several countries at the top of the life evaluation ranking also carry some of the highest suicide rates in the world. This map puts that tension in view rather than leaving it outside the frame.

None of this says the World Happiness Report is wrong. It offers a second reading, with the calculation shown in full so anyone can check it.

How it is calculated

The two quantities cannot be compared as they are: one runs from 0 to 10, the other counts deaths per 100,000 people. Both are therefore turned into percentiles within the group of countries that have both figures — 142 of them — where 100 is the best in the group and 0 the worst. 

p_life    = percentile(life evaluation 0–10)
p_suicide = percentile(−rate per 100,000)

value = (1 − w) × p_life + w × p_suicide

With w = 0.25, suicide mortality carries a quarter of the weight. At w = 0 the result is exactly the World Happiness Report ranking.

Percentiles rather than a min–max scale, because min–max gets crushed by outliers: a single country far above the mean would squeeze every other into a narrow band. And not a ratio of the two, because a country with very few suicides would shoot to the top by arithmetic alone, regardless of how it feels to live there.

Three limits, stated

Suicide data is not equally reliable everywhere. Religious and legal stigma, coroner practice and the quality of death registers vary a great deal between countries. The World Health Organization estimates correct for under-reporting, but they remain estimates.

The 25% weight is a choice, not a finding. No theory sets how much suicide mortality should weigh inside a measure of wellbeing. The figure is arbitrary, and should be defended as such.

One state is left out on purpose. Israel is not represented on this map. Its figures exist and the sources carry them; showing them has been withheld on political and humanitarian grounds, in response to the genocide in Gaza. The exclusion happens before the percentiles are computed, so no country's position is shaped by a figure the map refuses to show — which also means every position here is one place different from what a 143-country sample would give. It is an editorial decision, and it is stated here rather than hidden.

The two sources cover different years. The life evaluation is an average of 2023–2025; the mortality estimates are for 2021. Four years separate the two, and nothing here bridges them.

The GHE estimates are used rather than the raw Mortality Database, because the former already correct for under-reporting and ill-defined causes of death, and so compare across countries.

A note on tone

Half of what this map draws is deaths. They are not a score, not a performance, and no country here wins or loses. What the map shows is the difference between two indicators — nothing more.
